1. Basics: Subject, Verb & Their Relationship

What is a Subject?

Subject = the noun/pronoun we are talking about. It comes before the verb.

Examples: Ram, She, The students, My house

What is a Verb?

A Verb shows one of three things:
  1. Action — He dances.
  2. State of Being — He is a teacher.
  3. Possession — He has a car.

The Golden Rule of SVA

Core Principle
The verb always agrees with its subject — no matter how far apart they are in the sentence.

This is Subject-Verb Agreement (SVA). If the subject is singular, the verb must be singular. If the subject is plural, the verb must be plural.

SVA is the most tested grammar topic in competitive exams. Out of every 5 error-correction questions, at least 2 come from SVA.

⚠️ Important: Only Nouns & Pronouns are Subjects

Adjectives and other parts of speech are never the subject. Only a noun or pronoun can be the subject.

Consistent and focused effort leads to success.
👉 "Consistent" and "focused" are adjectives. The subject is only "effort" (singular) → verb = leads (singular). Do not apply the "AND" rule here!

2. Singular vs. Plural Verbs — Quick Reference

🔵 Singular Verbs

(Used with singular subjects: He, She, It, Ram, etc.)

  • V1 + s / es   (dances, goes)
  • is
  • was
  • has
  • does
🟢 Plural Verbs

(Used with plural subjects: They, We, You, Students, etc.)

  • V1 without s/es   (dance, go)
  • are
  • were
  • have
  • do

The Special Case of "I"

"I" is a unique subject that doesn't follow the standard singular/plural split:
  • I dance (no s/es — acts like plural)
  • I have (acts like plural)
  • I am (unique — neither is nor are)
  • I was (acts like singular)

⚠️ S/ES only goes with V1 (Base Form)

S/ES can ONLY be added to the base form (V1) of a verb.
It cannot be added to V2, V3, or V1+ing:
wents (V2 + s)    gones (V3 + s)    goings (V1+ing + s)
goes (V1 + s/es)
1 Concept 1 — The "AND" Rule

Part A: Two Different Subjects → Plural Verb

Rule
When two different subjects are connected by "and" → use a PLURAL verb.
Ram and Shyam ARE good friends.
Sita and Geeta ARE my cousins.
My laptop, phone and notebook ARE on the table.
My sister and I HAVE finished the work.

Part B: "AND" but One Idea → Singular Verb

Rule
When the two subjects joined by "and" together represent one single idea, one dish, one vehicle, or one concept → use a SINGULAR verb.

Key question to ask: "Are these truly two separate things, or do they come together as one unit?"

📝 Worked Examples — Rajma & Rice
Rajma and rice ___ my favourite food.
→ Eaten together as one dish? Yes!
IS
Rajma and rice ___ bought from the grocery shop.
→ Bought in separate packets? Yes!
ARE
Rajma and rice ___ grown in different parts of India.
→ Two different crops? Yes!
ARE
Rajma and rice of Haldiram ___ very popular.
→ Served as one dish? Yes!
IS
🧠 The 90% Rule: Always go with what 90% of people do in real life. Don't base your answer on exceptional edge cases (e.g., someone who eats rajma and rice separately).
📝 More Classic Examples
Horse and cart ___ standing there.
→ A single vehicle (tanga)? Yes!
IS
Peanut butter and jelly ___ a classic sandwich filling.
→ One sandwich filling? Yes!
IS
The sun and moon ___ heavenly bodies.
→ Two separate celestial objects? Yes!
ARE
Bread and butter can be BOTH singular or plural depending on context:
  • As a meal eaten together → IS (one breakfast item)
  • As two products bought separately → ARE
  • As a phrase meaning "livelihood" → IS (e.g., "This is my bread and butter.")

Part C: Use Articles to Detect — One Person or Two?

When there is ambiguity about whether we are talking about one person or two different people, check the article before the second subject.

Sentence Pattern Interpretation Verb
The teacher and the singer… Two different people (article used before 2nd subject) ARE / HAVE
The teacher and singer… One person playing two roles (no article before 2nd subject) IS / HAS
A poet and a dancer… Two different people (article 'a' used before 2nd subject) ARE / HAVE
A poet and dancer… One person (poet who is also a dancer) IS / HAS
⚠️ IMPORTANT: Apply the Article Rule only when there is genuine ambiguity. When the sentence already makes it clear the subjects are different people (e.g., teacher & student, brother & sister, mother & child, manager & employee), you do not need to check the article — always use a plural verb.
📝 Article Rule — Practice
The manager and the supervisor ___ approved the proposal.
HAVE
The manager and supervisor ___ approved the proposal.
HAS
The author and illustrator ___ written this book.
HAS
The writer, editor and publisher ___ done this.
HAS
The teacher and student ___ reached school on time.
→ Clearly different people — no article check needed!
HAVE
The brother and sister ___ arrived.
→ Clearly different people — no article check needed!
HAVE

Part D: Two Articles before Adjectives = Two Different Nouns

If two articles appear before two adjectives joined by "and", they signal two separate nouns, even if only one noun is written.

A bright and an exotic flower were blooming.
→ Two articles = two different flowers (one is bright, one is exotic) → WERE
A bright and exotic flower ___ blooming.
→ One article = one flower that is both bright and exotic → WAS
PatternMeaningVerb
A bright and an exotic flower Two different flowers WERE / HAVE BEEN
A bright and exotic flower One flower (two qualities) WAS / HAS BEEN
An adventurous and a daring explorer Two different explorers WERE / ARE
An adventurous and daring explorer One explorer (two qualities) WAS / IS

Part E: Exception — EACH / EVERY with AND

Exception Rule
If EACH or EVERY is used before subjects joined by "and", the verb is always SINGULAR — regardless of how many subjects there are.
Each boy and each girl IS talented.
Every student and every teacher IS present.
Each boy and each girl ARE talented.
EACH and EVERY are inherently singular words. They override the "AND = plural" rule.

Part F: Adjectives Joined by AND — Not Two Subjects!

Remember: Only nouns and pronouns are subjects. If adjectives are joined by "and", there is still only one subject.
A beautiful and vibrant painting hangs on the wall.
→ "beautiful" and "vibrant" are adjectives. Subject = painting (singular) → hangs

An honest and kind girl lives next door.
→ "honest" and "kind" are adjectives. Subject = girl (singular) → lives

📌 AND Rule — Full Summary

SituationVerb Type
Two truly different subjects joined by AND✅ Plural
Two subjects = one idea / one thing✅ Singular
Two different people (clear from context)✅ Plural
One person with two roles (no article before 2nd subject)✅ Singular
Two people confirmed by article before 2nd subject✅ Plural
EACH / EVERY + and + subjects✅ Singular (always)
Two adjectives + AND + one noun✅ Singular
Two articles + two adjectives joined by AND✅ Plural
2 Concept 2 — OR / EITHER-OR / NEITHER-NOR / NOT ONLY…BUT ALSO
The Nearest Subject Rule
When two subjects are connected by OR / EITHER…OR / NEITHER…NOR / NOT ONLY…BUT ALSO, the verb agrees with the nearest (closest) subject.
💡 Think of it this way: the verb is "tired of long-distance relationships" and prefers the subject closest to it!
📝 Worked Examples
Either Ramesh or his friends ___ waiting outside.
→ Nearest subject = "friends" (plural)
ARE
Not only the teacher but also the students ___ very talented.
→ Nearest subject = "students" (plural)
ARE
Either she or her mother ___ done it.
→ Nearest subject = "mother" (singular)
HAS
Neither Sonia nor her family members ___ at the party.
→ Nearest subject = "members" (plural)
ARE / DANCE
The connector words OR, EITHER-OR, NEITHER-NOR, NOT ONLY-BUT ALSO all follow the same rule: verb matches the nearest subject.
3 Concept 3 — AS WELL AS / WITH / TOGETHER WITH / APART FROM…
The First Subject Rule
When subjects are connected by AS WELL AS / WITH / TOGETHER WITH / IN ADDITION TO / APART FROM / LIKE / UNLIKE / RATHER THAN / ALONG WITH, the verb agrees with the first (farther) subject.
💡 Think of it this way: the second subject is just "company" — the first subject is the real boss! "Long-distance love never fades."
ConnectorHindi MeaningVerb follows…

📝 Worked Examples
Ram as well as his friends ___ talented.
→ First subject = "Ram" (singular)
IS
The chalk together with the boxes ___ kept here.
→ First subject = "chalk" (singular)
IS
The teacher as well as the students ___ excited.
→ First subject = "teacher" (singular)
IS
The employees along with the manager ___ contributed.
→ First subject = "employees" (plural)
HAVE
John rather than his colleagues ___ attending the seminar.
→ First subject = "John" (singular)
IS
Key contrast: OR/EITHER-OR/NEITHER-NOR → Nearest subject | AS WELL AS / WITH / ALONG WITH → First subject.

🔀 What if EACH/EVERY appears with AS WELL AS?

When "as well as" is used, only the first subject matters. If that first subject has "each" or "every", the verb is singular. If it's plural, the verb is plural. Don't worry about what appears after "as well as."
Each student as well as my friends ___ happy today.
→ First subject = "each student" (singular) → IS

My friends as well as each student ___ happy today.
→ First subject = "my friends" (plural) → ARE
4 Concept 4 — EACH / EVERY / EITHER / NEITHER (as Adjectives)
Rule 1 of 120 Grammar Rules
When EACH / EVERY / EITHER / NEITHER is used directly before a noun (as an adjective), both the noun AND the verb are SINGULAR.
WordMeaningExample
Each हर एक / प्रत्येक (one by one) Each student is hardworking.
Every हर / प्रत्येक (all together) Every student is hardworking.
Either दो में से एक (one of two) Either student is hardworking.
Neither दो में से कोई नहीं (none of two) Neither student is lazy.

With "OF" — EACH OF / EITHER OF / NEITHER OF

EACH OF / EITHER OF / NEITHER OF can be followed by a plural noun, but the verb remains SINGULAR.

⚠️ EVERY OF is grammatically WRONG. "Every" is only an adjective, not a pronoun. Use Everyone of instead.
📝 Examples
Each of the students ___ smart.
IS
Either of the students ___ hardworking.
IS
Neither of the students ___ foolish.
IS
Every of the students IS smart.
WRONG
Everyone of the students IS smart.
CORRECT
The noun after EACH OF / EITHER OF / NEITHER OF is always plural (because we're talking about a group), but the verb is always singular. This is a very common exam trap!
