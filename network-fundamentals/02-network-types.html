Network Types

Section 2.1

Classification Overview

Computer networks are classified based on several criteria. The most common classification is by geographic coverage — the physical area the network spans. Other factors include ownership (private vs public), transmission speed, and the technology used.

Section 2.2

LAN – Local Area Network

LAN Local Area Network
Geographic Span
Up to 1–2 km
Typical Speed
10 Mbps – 10 Gbps
Ownership
Private (single organisation)
Error Rate
Very Low

A Local Area Network (LAN) is a network that connects computers and devices in a limited geographic area such as a home, school, office building, or small group of buildings. It is typically owned, controlled, and managed by a single organization or individual.

Characteristics of LAN

  • High Speed: Modern LANs operate at 100 Mbps (Fast Ethernet), 1 Gbps (Gigabit Ethernet), or even 10 Gbps in data centers.
  • Short Distance: Typically spans a single floor, building, or campus block (up to 1 km).
  • Low Cost: Relatively cheap to install and maintain compared to MAN or WAN.
  • Low Error Rate: Due to short distance, data integrity is high with minimal transmission errors.
  • Private Ownership: The organisation itself owns and manages the network.
  • Broadcast Domain: All devices share the same broadcast domain (in traditional LANs before VLANs).

Technologies Used

  • Ethernet (IEEE 802.3): Most common wired LAN standard using twisted-pair cables (Cat5e, Cat6) or fiber optics.
  • Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11): Wireless LAN using radio waves. Standards: 802.11n (Wi-Fi 4), 802.11ac (Wi-Fi 5), 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6).
  • Token Ring (IEEE 802.5): Older technology, largely replaced by Ethernet.
  • FDDI: Fiber Distributed Data Interface — used in backbone LANs.

Examples of LAN

  • Home network connecting PCs, laptops, smartphones, and smart TV via a Wi-Fi router
  • Office network connecting 50 computers, printers, and a file server
  • Computer lab in a school or university
  • Hospital ward network for patient monitoring systems

Advantages

  • Very high speed (Gbps)
  • Low error rate and latency
  • Easy to set up and manage
  • Cost-effective for small areas
  • Secure (private ownership)

Disadvantages

  • Limited geographic coverage
  • Number of devices limited by hardware
  • Wired LANs restrict mobility
  • Congestion if too many devices

Section 2.3

MAN – Metropolitan Area Network

MAN Metropolitan Area Network
Geographic Span
5 – 50 km (city-wide)
Typical Speed
1 Mbps – 1 Gbps
Ownership
ISP or Government
Example
Cable TV, city Wi-Fi

A Metropolitan Area Network (MAN) is a network that spans a city or a large campus. It is larger than a LAN but smaller than a WAN. MANs are typically operated by an Internet Service Provider (ISP) or a government body to interconnect several LANs within a metropolitan area.

Characteristics of MAN

  • City-scale coverage: Spans 5–50 km, covering a city, town, or large campus.
  • Higher cost than LAN: Requires leased lines or fiber infrastructure across the city.
  • Moderate-high speed: Typically 1 Mbps to 1 Gbps, though modern fiber MANs can be faster.
  • Usually managed by an ISP: Individual organisations lease bandwidth rather than owning the infrastructure.
  • Connects multiple LANs: Different office branches of a bank across a city use a MAN to communicate.

Technologies Used

  • DQDB (Distributed Queue Dual Bus): IEEE 802.6 standard for MANs.
  • ATM (Asynchronous Transfer Mode): Used in older MAN deployments.
  • WiMAX (IEEE 802.16): Wireless broadband for metropolitan coverage.
  • Fiber Optic: Modern MANs use fiber for high-speed city-wide connectivity.
  • SONET/SDH: Synchronous Optical Networking used in MAN backbones.

Examples of MAN

  • Cable TV network running through a city
  • City Wi-Fi (free Wi-Fi across a municipality)
  • Bank branches connected across a city through leased fiber lines
  • Mumbai or Delhi metropolitan fiber backbone connecting government offices

Advantages

  • Covers an entire city
  • Higher speed than WAN
  • Connects multiple LANs easily
  • Can support thousands of users

Disadvantages

  • Expensive to install and maintain
  • Difficult to manage
  • Security risks over wider area
  • Dependent on ISP infrastructure

Section 2.4

WAN – Wide Area Network

WAN Wide Area Network
Geographic Span
Country to Global
Typical Speed
Kbps – Gbps
Ownership
Telecom / ISP (public)
Example
The Internet, SWIFT

A Wide Area Network (WAN) spans large geographical distances — countries, continents, or the entire globe. The best known example is the Internet. WANs connect multiple LANs and MANs together using technologies like leased lines, satellite links, fiber optic undersea cables, and public telephone networks.

Characteristics of WAN

  • Global coverage: Can span countries, continents, or the entire world.
  • Variable speed: Historically slow (dial-up at 56 Kbps), but modern WANs use fiber (Tbps backbone).
  • Higher latency: Greater propagation delay due to long physical distances.
  • Public infrastructure: Uses public telecommunications infrastructure (phone lines, undersea cables, satellites).
  • Higher error rate: More susceptible to noise and interference than LANs.
  • Expensive: High installation, leasing, and maintenance costs.
  • Complex management: Requires specialist routers, MPLS, SD-WAN technologies.

Technologies Used

  • Leased Lines (T1, T3, E1): Dedicated point-to-point links rented from a carrier.
  • MPLS (Multi-Protocol Label Switching): Modern WAN technology for fast and reliable routing.
  • Frame Relay: Older WAN packet-switching technology (largely obsolete).
  • ATM (Asynchronous Transfer Mode): Fixed-size cell switching for WAN backbones.
  • Satellite Links: Used for remote areas — high latency but global coverage.
  • Undersea Fiber Cables: Intercontinental backbone carrying billions of bits per second.
  • SD-WAN: Software-Defined WAN — modern approach using internet + software for flexible WANs.

Examples of WAN

  • The Internet: The largest and most famous WAN, connecting billions of devices globally.
  • SWIFT Network: The global financial messaging network connecting banks worldwide.
  • Multi-national Corporate Networks: Company branches in USA, UK, and India connected via MPLS WAN.
  • Military Networks: Classified networks spanning countries and oceans.

Section 2.5

PAN – Personal Area Network

PAN Personal Area Network
Geographic Span
~10 meters (personal space)
Typical Speed
Bluetooth: ~3 Mbps; USB: 480 Mbps
Ownership
Individual person
Technologies
Bluetooth, ZigBee, USB, IrDA

A Personal Area Network (PAN) is the smallest type of network, typically limited to a person's immediate workspace or body area — within about 10 meters. A PAN connects personal devices like a smartphone, laptop, smartwatch, wireless earphones, and tablet belonging to a single person.

Types of PAN

  • Wired PAN: Using USB or FireWire to connect devices like a phone to a PC.
  • Wireless PAN (WPAN): Using Bluetooth (IEEE 802.15.1), ZigBee (IEEE 802.15.4), or IrDA (infrared).

Technologies Used

  • Bluetooth (IEEE 802.15.1): Most common. Used for headphones, keyboards, file transfer (~10m range, up to 3 Mbps).
  • ZigBee (IEEE 802.15.4): Low-power, short-range, used in smart home and IoT sensors.
  • IrDA: Infrared Data Association — older technology, line-of-sight required.
  • NFC (Near Field Communication): Very short range (~4 cm), used for contactless payments.
  • USB / USB-C / Thunderbolt: Wired PAN connections for high-speed device communication.

Examples of PAN

  • Connecting a Bluetooth headset to your smartphone
  • Syncing a smartwatch with your phone
  • Connecting a wireless keyboard and mouse to a laptop
  • Transferring files between phone and laptop via Bluetooth
  • Apple AirDrop using Wi-Fi Direct and Bluetooth

Section 2.6

CAN – Campus Area Network

CAN Campus Area Network
Geographic Span
1 – 5 km (campus)
Typical Speed
100 Mbps – 10 Gbps
Ownership
University / Company
Example
University / corporate campus

A Campus Area Network (CAN) — also called a Corporate Area Network — connects multiple LANs within a limited geographic area such as a university campus, military base, or large corporate office complex. It is larger than a single LAN but smaller than a MAN.

Characteristics of CAN

  • Multi-building coverage: Connects buildings across a campus — academic blocks, hostels, labs, admin buildings.
  • Privately owned: The institution or company owns and manages the entire infrastructure.
  • High speed backbone: Typically uses Gigabit Ethernet or fiber optic backbone between buildings.
  • Interconnects multiple LANs: Each department or building has its own LAN, and these are all interconnected via the CAN.
  • Shared internet gateway: All devices share the campus internet connection through a border router.

Technologies Used

  • Gigabit Ethernet (IEEE 802.3): For backbone connections between buildings.
  • Fiber Optic Cables: For high-speed inter-building links.
  • Layer 3 Switches / Routers: For routing between department LANs within the campus.
  • Wireless (Wi-Fi 6): Campuses deploy dense Wi-Fi Access Points (APs) for mobility.
  • VLANs: Virtual LANs to logically separate department traffic on the shared physical infrastructure.

Examples of CAN

  • IIT Bombay's campus network connecting all departments, hostels, and labs
  • Google's corporate campus in Mountain View, California
  • Military base network connecting barracks, command centres, and armory
  • Hospital campus connecting OPD, ICU, radiology, and administrative buildings
